# Who helps whom

> [!question] Questions of this page
> - What has the notebook found about cooperation, in every page that looked
>   for it?
> - Where does help exist in these worlds, and why so little of it?
> - What has been tried to make helping pay, and what happened?
> - What would have to change for cooperation to evolve?

Cooperation is the oldest question of evolution theory: how can a behaviour
that costs the one who does it, and benefits another, be selected? There are
five known answers — kin selection, direct reciprocity (I help you, you help
me later), indirect reciprocity (I help whoever has a good name), groups
competing as groups, and the network keeping helpers together
([Do agents cooperate?](do-agents-cooperate.md) explains each). Several pages of
this notebook have looked for one of them. This page puts what they found
side by side.

## What helping is, here

A world of this notebook has one [game](one-iteration.md#phase-2-the-game "The second phase of an iteration: every agent stakes all its tokens on its own node and its neighbours', and every node goes to someone."): every [agent](the-world.md#agents-and-tokens "One member of a world: it lives on a node of the network, holds tokens and has a brain that takes all its decisions.") stakes [tokens](the-world.md#agents-and-tokens "The one thing of value in a world: every agent holds a whole number of them, and their total never changes.") on its own node
and its [neighbours](the-world.md#the-network "An agent joined to another by a connection.")' nodes, and whoever stakes most on a node takes it. So what
could helping be? [Gliders, and who helps whom](gliders-and-who-helps-whom.md) answered by
the rules, not by guessing from outcomes: **a stake helps** the holder of a
node if, without it, the holder would have lost the node. That is counted
exactly, for every stake of every game (the note [Help](do-agents-cooperate.md#help)).
Comparing the helped with the rest does not work: those who are helped are
those in danger, so the comparison measures need, not benefit.

## Why so little

Three reasons recur.

**The rules leave no room for help.** In a game where the largest stake on a
node takes it, a stake on someone else's node is an attack unless the rules
say otherwise. B1's revolutions say otherwise by accident (a revolutionary
stake can keep a node for its holder), and so B1 has help as a by-product. B2
has hardly any revolutions, and so hardly any help. Wherever help existed in
these worlds, a rule made it possible: revolutions, pooled sides, alliances.

**There is no future to reciprocate in.** Reciprocity needs the same two
agents to meet again. In these worlds a node changes hands constantly, and the
brain that was helped is often gone before it could return the favour
([Do agents cooperate?](do-agents-cooperate.md)). Even where a pair lasts, a lasting
mutual stake is more often a feud than a partnership
([Gliders, and who helps whom](gliders-and-who-helps-whom.md)).

**Seeing is not enough.** Giving brains the information — kin sense, who
helped me, where my brain came from — did not by itself create help. A brain
can read what it is told; whether reading it pays depends on the rules. Kin
sense created groups only where a rule pooled the stakes of kin; an input
naming the helper changed nothing where helping itself was rare.

## What would have to change

For cooperation to evolve in these worlds, the evidence points to rules rather
than brains:

1. **A stake that can defend another's node**, without being a free gift: a
   support stake that counts only if it is returned (mutual), or that buys a
   share of what it defends. Plain support stakes were given up; alliances,
   which make support mutual by consent, were taken up at once.
2. **An identity that outlives a lost node**, so that a debt can be paid back:
   an agent whose brain survives being conquered (a "came from" input was a
   first, insufficient step).
3. **A reason to stay**: in B2 the flow runs one way because links do not have
   to be used ([Lightning in every world](where-the-tokens-flow.md#lightning-in-every-world)).
   Where links must carry tokens to survive, agents stake on each other — the
   raw material of exchange is already there.

Alliances show that a rule which makes help mutual is adopted quickly. What
they did not show is [open-ended evolution](open-ended-test.md "Evolution that keeps producing new things without end, not only up to a plateau."): one convention spreads, the world
becomes peaceful, and conquest — the engine of selection here — nearly stops.
The open question is a rule under which cooperation is adopted *and*
competition between groups goes on.
