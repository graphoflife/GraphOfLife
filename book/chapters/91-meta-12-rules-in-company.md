# Meta XII · Rules in company

> [!question] Questions of this chapter
> - Part V threw the book's rules into B2 one at a time. What does a rule do
>   when other rules are thrown with it?
> - Which rules matter whatever their company, and which only alone?
> - Does any combination come nearer to a world that keeps evolving?

Most chapters of Part V asked what one rule does to B2. In October 2026 the
author asked for the rules in combination, and six pilot batches answered:
four played one rule together with each of the others in turn — brains mixed
at birth (batch Y, [Chapter 82](82-mixing-with-every-other-rule.md)), no
revolutions (W, [Chapter 88](88-revolutions-with-every-other-rule.md)),
handover (X, [Chapter 89](89-handover-with-every-other-rule.md)) and gifts
(T, [Chapter 90](90-gifts-with-every-other-rule.md)) — and two threw eight
rules at once, in sixteen worlds each, on B2 and on B1 (batches U and V,
[Chapter 86](86-eight-switches-at-once.md)). Every world is a pilot of 10,000
tokens played for 1,000 iterations — the four batches of pairs in two seeds,
the two of eight switches in one — and every one read the same way at
iteration 1,000. This chapter gathers what they say together.

## Alone is not in company

The plainest lesson is a warning. **What a rule does alone in B2 is often not
what it does among other rules.**

- **Mixing's riches.** Alone, brains mixed at birth take a third to a half of
  B2's agents out of poverty. Paired with one other rule they survive sixteen
  of 23 (Chapter 82); among the sixteen worlds of batch U, with four rules on
  in most, they all but vanish — 0.07 off the share of the poor on average
  (Chapter 86). Among the companions that undo them are the estate to the
  newborns or the parents, which pays for births; handover (48 and 32 more
  poor in every hundred, Chapter 89); and, a little, gifts (11 and 17,
  Chapter 90). And one companion they need: without revolutions, mixed
  worlds grow and grow poor (Chapter 88).
- **Gifts' speed.** Alone, gifts made B2's brains of 1,000 beat those of 500
  more clearly than in B2 itself; with another rule as well, no more than B2
  does (Chapter 90).
- **Links that hold a token** take 0.29 and 0.33 off B2's share of the poor
  alone, and 0.06 among the sixteen; **every unused link lapsing** halves its
  fall in dimension; **no revolutions** on B1 does not do in company what it
  does alone.

The reason is that rules act together. Batch U's chains of pairs — the
effects of two rules acting jointly, which its sixteen worlds can measure in
sums of four — are as large as most single rules' effects (Chapter 86). A
rule measured alone tells what it does in B2; to know what it does, it has to
be met in company.

## Three kinds of rules

**Rules of rare events are details.** Revolutions win about one node in
three hundred a game in B2 (a median of 0.24 per hundred agents an
iteration), and gifts move one to four thousandths of the tokens a game.
Switching either changes almost nothing whatever else the rules are: over
their 24 and 21 companions, the median change in the share of the poor is
+0.002 for both. The exceptions are the worlds where these events are common
or count — mixed worlds, small and rich, where revolutions are five to ten
times as common and hold the world small.

**Rules that reshape the network do so everywhere.** Handover costs two marks
of the health bar with nearly every companion, thinning the network into
trees on single links, and unfolds it a little (Chapter 89). Every unused
link lapsing makes worlds rich, small and about two-dimensional, and costs
marks too (Chapters 86 and 87).

**Rules that set a world's size set it in any company.** The estate of the
dead paid to the newborns or the parents fills a world — about 1,000 agents
more among the sixteen on B2, 1,400 on B1 — and makes it poorer and more
alike, on both baselines, alone and in company (Chapters 73 and 86). Links
that hold a token shrink it. Who mates sets it too: with only parents as
mates, all ten mixed worlds of Experiment 19 settled at about 2,000 agents
([Chapter 85](85-mixed-at-birth-with-parents-as-mates.md)). A world's size,
Meta XI found, is set by how eagerly its agents breed; these are the rules
that change that.

## Does evolution go on?

In lifetime contests, the brains of iteration 1,000 against those of 500,
**the later brains win in 174 of the 198 worlds** of the five batches on B2. The
medians differ by batch: 0.69 with mixing, 0.64 without revolutions and with
gifts, 0.60 among the sixteen of batch U, 0.57 with handover — against 0.68
for B2's ten worlds alone. It goes slowest with no old age, with the estate to
the newborns and with the last token kept at home, and fastest with brain
units that hold a token and with brains that grow. No combination stopped it
outright; none made it run away.

**On B1, no combination starts it again.** Among the sixteen worlds of batch V
the later brains won in six of the fourteen that lived, with a median of
0.46 — as in B1 itself, whose brains of 1,000 held a median of 0.49 against
those of 500 ([Chapter 46](46-how-to-tell-a-world-that-keeps-evolving.md)).
No switch moved that beyond the wide spread of the chains of pairs — not
even two of B2's own rules, brains that change only when copied and parents
that keep their links (Chapter 86).

## Nearer the author's picture?

The author's picture is a world that is a space of about three dimensions,
whose network no single loss can cut, and whose agents are many-sided — not
all of them holding one to three tokens ([Meta X](79-meta-10-healthy-and-rich.md)).
None of these combinations is all of that. The best of the sixteen on B2 —
no revolutions, no random inputs, links that hold a token and mixing — meets
every mark of the health bar with a quarter of its agents poor and a
diversity score of 0.68, but its network is folded: a typical agent reaches
half the world in 1.19 times the steps its random twin needs, as in nearly
every B2 world ([Chapter 87](87-what-folds-a-world.md)).

Read together at iteration 1,000, 161 of the 466 worlds measured then are
spaces at least half again as wide as their random twins
(`research/atlas/census.py`). All but eleven have one of five rules, and each
rule pays for the room in its own coin (the medians of the worlds that are
spaces):

| rule | worlds that are spaces | agents with 3 tokens or fewer | marks (of 7) | dimension |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| the estate of the dead paid to births | 33 | 90% | 7 | 3.5 |
| no old age | 22 | 93% | 7 | 3.4 |
| handover | 25 | 79% | 4 | 3.8 |
| every unused link lapsing | 26 | 7% | 4 | 2.2 |
| deaths not mended, as in B1 | 44 | 51% | 4 | 2.6 |

The first three fill a world with the poor; lapsing links leave it rich, but
about two-dimensional and short of marks; where deaths are not mended, half
the agents are poor and the network is easily cut. Of the eleven
others, four mixed worlds come nearer, each in one seed: with a slower change
of brains, with the estate to the poorest, with an input that tells where a
stake came from, and with no random inputs. Each is 1.5 to 2.4 times as wide
as its twin, meets every mark at about three dimensions, and holds about half
its agents at three tokens or fewer, with diversity scores of 0.48 to 0.63.
But they seem to be passing through. In their second seeds, two of the four
rules grew a world of 3,500 agents, four in five of them poor, and one a
world of 290, rich and folded (1.15); only the input of where a stake came
from gave nearly the same world twice (1.52 and 1.49, about half poor in
both). And played on to 10,000 iterations, two of the four worlds drifted
out of the picture: with the estate to the poorest, the world grew to 2,300
agents, 69% of them poor, and its diversity score fell to 0.28, though it
stayed the widest of the long runs (1.79); with no random inputs, it shrank
to 370 agents, rich and folded (1.26), its diversity kept (0.63). The
came-from world's second seed shrank too, to 720 agents and 1.24; its first
waits its turn.

> [!info] Added later (19 October 2026): too even, not too poor
> The author's worry was a world whose agents hold their tokens too evenly —
> almost everyone the same few — and the share at three tokens or fewer only
> stood in for it ([Meta X](79-meta-10-healthy-and-rich.md)). Read with the
> [Gini coefficient](../notes/gini-coefficient.md) of the agents' tokens, the census turns round.
> - **The spaces that "pay with poverty" hold their tokens unevenly.** Their
>   median Gini is 0.45 with the estate paid to births, 0.58 without old age
>   and 0.60 with handover. B2's worlds read 0.49 to 0.56, and a random
>   exchange of tokens would leave 0.33 to 0.42 at their two to four tokens per
>   agent.
> - **The rich spaces are the even ones.** With every unused link lapsing the
>   median Gini is 0.25, at seven tokens per agent where random exchange
>   would leave 0.46; where deaths are not mended it is 0.39.
> - **The four mixed worlds that came nearer are no more even than B2** (0.55
>   to 0.60): the half of their agents at three tokens or fewer no longer
>   counts against them.
>
> So room costs evenness only where links lapse. What the spaces of births,
> no old age and handover still miss is diversity, or marks. At 10,000
> iterations it is the small rich worlds that grew even
> ([Chapter 92](92-the-candidates-played-on.md)), the second seed without
> random inputs among them (291 agents, 34 tokens per agent and a Gini of
> 0.39 already at 1,000).

## What this says

- **Measure rules in company.** A rule's effect alone is one corner of a
  table; many rules change sign or size among others.
- **Mixing is the rule to build on, with care**: at iteration 1,000 its rise
  in diversity holds with nearly every companion, its riches only where
  nothing pays for births, nothing passes links on, and revolutions are left
  on.
- **The rules that matter most are the rules of birth** — who pays for it,
  who may breed, who mates — for they set a world's size, its riches, and,
  through who dies with how many links, its geometry.
- **Room and riches pull apart, and so do life and riches.** Every rule
  that keeps a world a space pays for the room with poverty, a flat network
  or cuts, and the mixed worlds that were spaces, healthy and half rich at
  iteration 1,000 did not stay so. When Experiment 19 took mixing's free mate
  away, every world lived to 20,000 iterations, at about 2,000 agents, six in
  ten of them poor. What is still missing is the pull Meta XI asked for:
  births that pay more when few are born and less when many are.
